Fraud & Risk · Field Note

Reading a ₹1.5 crore loan the way a model does

By Pallav Pradhan · ~5 min read

A home-loan proposal landed on my desk for roughly ₹1.5 crore — a figure that, on its own, already put the borrower well past the comfort zone of a single asset. The paperwork was clean. The income documentation looked fine. A traditional scorecard would have leaned on the three bureau numbers and called it good. That's exactly where I stopped trusting the paperwork and started reading the cash flow instead.

The pattern I was looking for is what I call circular transactions: money moving between a small set of linked accounts or parties in a loop, so that inflows look like income and outflows look like spending, when in reality the same money is just going round and round. Individually, every line is plausible. The fraud is in the relationship between the lines — and that's the part a single bureau score will never see, because a score is a number, not a graph.

“Credit risk is a graph problem, not a score problem. A score is a single coordinate; cash flow is the shape of the whole neighbourhood.”

Why the scorecard is the wrong instrument

This is the core argument I keep making: credit risk is a graph problem, not a score problem. A borrower isn't a FICO digit. They're a node connected to employers, other lenders, fintech and BNPL accounts, guarantors, and sometimes to other applicants in the same file. The risk lives in the edges — in the undisclosed liability here, the stacked obligation there, the revenue that turns out to be the customer's own money coming back out the other end. What a bureau tells you is a single coordinate. What cash-flow and transaction analysis tells you is the shape of the whole neighbourhood.

What the model is actually doing

The reason I'm so insistent on the “machine” in this is that this is precisely the work a model does well and a human does badly: not making the final call, but scanning hundreds of transaction links for the loop that a tired, time-pressed analyst will miss. I didn't flag this file with a black-box that spat out “fraud.” I flagged it because the transaction graph looked circular, I traced it by hand, and it was. The model's job is to make that tracing systematic and fast — and to keep the bank honest with itself about where the real risk is, before the money goes out the door.

The gap where the money is lost

The loan was treated differently because of what the cash flow actually showed, not because of what a score suggested. That gap — between the number a scorecard produces and the picture the transactions paint — is where most of the money that banks lose to “good paper” is hiding. My work is to shrink that gap.

A model doesn't replace that judgement. It removes the blind spot: it sees the loop you would have missed under deadline, and it does it on every file, not just the ones that happen to feel off. That's the entire value proposition of bringing machine learning to credit — not a faster yes or no, but a sharper “what am I actually seeing here?”
